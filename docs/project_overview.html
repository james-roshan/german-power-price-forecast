German Day-Ahead Electricity Price Forecasting

Problem statement, data, methodology, and beneficiaries — a complete guide in plain language with examples.

1. The Core Question

Every day, the German electricity market runs a single auction. Buyers and sellers submit bids. Prices are set. The market closes. And then electricity flows.

The one question this project answers:
"What will electricity cost in Germany tomorrow — for each of the 24 hours of the day — before the auction closes today at noon?"

This is called a day-ahead price forecast. The output is 24 numbers, one per hour, in €/MWh (euros per megawatt-hour). These numbers are what factories, traders, and power plant operators use to plan their next day.

2. How the Electricity Market Works

Electricity cannot be stored cheaply at scale. Every second, the amount generated must exactly match the amount consumed. To coordinate this, Germany runs a day-ahead auction called EPEX SPOT.

The auction process

TimeWhat Happens
D−1, 09:00 Weather forecasts for day D are published. Market participants start building their bids.
D−1, 12:00 Auction closes. All bids must be submitted. The market algorithm matches supply and demand for each of the 24 hours of day D.
D−1, 12:42 Results published. 24 prices are fixed — one per hour. This is the day-ahead price.
Day D, 00:00 Electricity delivery begins. Generators produce, consumers consume, according to contracts settled at the auction price.
Why the price varies by hour: At 3 AM, most factories are closed, demand is low, renewables are producing → price is cheap. At 6 PM on a cold winter evening, everyone is cooking, heating, and factories are running → demand spikes, expensive gas plants turn on → price is high.

What drives the price

Think of electricity like a marketplace: price = what sellers need to be paid for the last unit of electricity to meet demand.

  • Solar is sunny: free electricity floods the grid → price drops, sometimes goes negative
  • Strong wind: wind turbines generate for near-zero cost → price falls
  • No wind, no sun: gas power plants must run → price rises (gas is expensive)
  • Public holiday: factories shut down → demand drops → price falls
  • Cold Monday morning: factories restart, heating peaks → demand spikes → price rises

4. What Data the Model Uses as Inputs

To make a prediction for Friday at 17:00, you can only use data that existed on Thursday morning before noon. This is the central rule.

✓ SAFE
Price at same hour last week (Wednesday 17:00)
168 hours ago — definitely known by Thursday morning
✓ SAFE
Weather forecast for Friday issued Thursday morning
Open-Meteo publishes forecasts daily — this is what you'd have at bid time
✓ SAFE
Calendar info: it's a Friday, no public holiday
Known infinitely in advance
✗ CHEAT
Actual solar generation on Friday at 12:00
You don't have this until Friday itself — using it inflates accuracy artificially
✗ CHEAT
Actual price at Friday 16:00 (to predict Friday 17:00)
The auction hasn't happened yet — this is the future
This is called look-ahead leakage. If you accidentally use future data, your model looks accurate in testing but fails completely in production. Preventing this is the most important engineering decision in the project.

Complete Feature Table

price_lag168h
Price at the exact same hour exactly 1 week ago. The strongest single predictor — electricity prices have strong weekly cycles.
price_lag48h
Price 2 days ago (same hour). 48h is the minimum safe lag — guaranteed to be known before the auction closes.
wind_speed_100m
Forecast wind speed at 100m height (where turbines sit) for 5 German locations. High wind = cheap electricity.
shortwave_radiation
Forecast solar radiation. High radiation = solar panels produce more = price falls. Especially powerful in summer afternoons.
hour_sin / hour_cos
The hour of day encoded as a circle (sin and cos), so the model knows hour 23 is close to hour 0 — which a plain number doesn't convey.
is_holiday
German public holiday flag. Holidays behave like Sundays: factories closed, demand low, prices lower.
residual_load_lag48h
Residual load = grid demand minus renewables, 2 days ago. When residual load is high, gas plants must run, price goes up.
price_roll168_mean
Rolling 7-day average price (computed from 48h-shifted data). Captures the current price regime — helps the model know if this is a generally expensive or cheap week.

5. Who Benefits and How

The 24-number forecast output is useful to multiple market participants. Each uses it differently.

🏭
Large Industrial Consumer
★ PRIMARY

Steel plants, cement factories, aluminium smelters consume thousands of MWh per day. They can shift flexible processes to cheap hours.

Forecast: 03:00 = €43/MWh
Forecast: 17:00 = €110/MWh
→ Run melting furnaces at 03:00
→ Reduce load at 17:00
Saving: ~€67/MWh × 5,000 MWh
= €335,000 saved per day
📈
Energy Trader
★ PRIMARY

Traders buy electricity cheap and sell it expensive, or hedge price risk for their clients. Accuracy = profit.

Market prices Friday 17:00 at €90
Your model says: €110/MWh
→ Trader buys contract at €90
→ Sells at auction for €110
Profit: €20/MWh × position size
🔋
Battery Storage Operator
★ PRIMARY

Batteries charge when electricity is cheap, discharge when expensive. Pure price arbitrage — the forecast directly determines when to charge vs discharge.

Cheap window: 03:00–06:00
→ Charge battery at €43/MWh
Expensive window: 17:00–19:00
→ Sell back at €110/MWh
Gross margin: €67/MWh
⚡
Gas Power Plant Owner
SECONDARY

Gas plants are expensive to run (fuel cost ~€80/MWh). They only run when the market price exceeds their running cost. The forecast tells them when to switch on.

Running cost: €80/MWh
Forecast 17:00: €110/MWh
→ Start plant at 16:00 (warm-up)
→ Sell at 17:00
Profit: €30/MWh × output
☀️
Wind / Solar Farm Owner
SECONDARY

Renewable generators can choose to store energy (if they have a battery) or sell directly. The forecast helps them decide when to sell vs when to hold.

Solar producing at 13:00 = €54
Forecast 17:00 = €110/MWh
→ Store energy from 13:00
→ Release to grid at 17:00
Extra revenue: €56/MWh
🏢
Utilities / Grid Operators
SECONDARY

Grid operators need to plan capacity — how much backup reserve to hold ready. A forecast of an expensive evening peak tells them to keep more gas capacity on standby.

Forecast: high evening spike
→ Hold extra reserve capacity
→ Avoid emergency imports
→ Prevent grid instability
Who does NOT benefit: Regular households pay a fixed retail electricity rate. The wholesale day-ahead price does not reach them directly — their price is set months or years in advance by their energy provider. This may change with smart meters and dynamic tariffs, but is not the case today for most consumers.

6. Why This Problem Is Technically Hard

This is not a standard regression problem. Three issues make it genuinely difficult:

Problem 1 — Look-ahead leakage

The most common mistake in energy forecasting. You only know forecasts of tomorrow's weather — not what the weather will actually be. Using actual observed weather looks like a small detail but inflates model accuracy by 30–50%. We run both versions and report the gap as an "oracle experiment."

Problem 2 — Non-stationarity

The electricity market in 2025 looks completely different from 2019. Solar capacity has tripled. Gas prices spiked in 2022 (Russia-Ukraine war) reaching 600+ €/MWh. A model trained on pre-2022 data will badly underestimate tail risk. You must decide explicitly: train through the crisis, exclude it, or down-weight it.

2022 price spike: From February 2022 to mid-2023, German electricity prices were 5–10× their historical average. A seasonal-naive model trained before 2022 would predict €80/MWh when the actual price was €500/MWh. This is not noise — it's a structural regime shift.

Problem 3 — Negative prices

On sunny, windy Sundays, Germany produces more electricity than it can consume. The price goes negative — producers pay consumers to take the electricity. This breaks standard forecasting assumptions: you cannot log-transform negative prices, and MAPE (a common metric) becomes meaningless near zero. We use MAE as the primary metric instead.

7. The Model Pipeline (Simplified)

The model is trained on 6 years of hourly data. For each delivery day in the test period, it is retrained only on data strictly before that day (rolling-origin validation). This simulates real deployment: you never peek at the future during training.

8. How We Measure Success

The baseline is the simplest possible forecast: use the price at the same hour one week ago. If our model cannot beat this, it has no value.

ModelMAE (€/MWh)vs Baseline
Seasonal Naive (same hour, 1 week ago) ~18–22 baseline
LEAR (Lasso on price lags only) ~14–17 ~20% better
LightGBM (lags + calendar) ~10–13 ~40% better
LightGBM (lags + calendar + weather) ~7–10 ~50% better
Oracle (same model, observed weather) ~4–7 ceiling — not achievable in production

Note: These are illustrative ranges based on published literature. Your actual numbers will vary. Always fill in real numbers from your own backtest before using them anywhere.

What the oracle gap means in interviews: The difference between the oracle (observed weather) and regular model (forecast weather) tells you exactly how much accuracy is lost because weather forecasts are imperfect. This is not a flaw in your model — it's a fundamental limit of the information available before the auction closes. Understanding and quantifying this gap is what separates a thoughtful ML engineer from one who just runs sklearn.
